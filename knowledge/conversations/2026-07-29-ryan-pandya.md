# Ryan Pandya — July 29, 2026

## Executive takeaway

Greenlit should help companies assemble evidence, coordinate readiness, and
prepare a strong preliminary dossier before final counsel review. It should not
position itself as replacing regulatory counsel.

Ryan validated structured intake, workspace coordination, preliminary drafting,
and gap analysis as plausible capabilities. His experience also suggests that
the most consequential delays may arise from process readiness, representative
batches, and safety studies rather than the act of writing.

## Direct observations from Ryan

- Perfect Day worked closely and sequentially with specialized counsel.
- The external firm had a defined process, timeline, price, and list of required
  materials, and guided the company through gaps and ambiguities.
- Perfect Day's internal team supplied process information and representative
  batch data; specialized third parties performed toxicology work when needed.
- The self-affirmed GRAS process took approximately two years by Ryan's recall,
  followed by FDA GRAS within roughly another year.
- A major stage gate was producing three non-consecutive manufacturing batches
  representative of the intended commercial process.
- Ongoing changes to process development, media, cell line, or downstream
  processing could prevent runs from being representative.
- Novel sequences or unfamiliar consumption levels may face a more difficult
  regulatory argument than a familiar protein made with a familiar process.
- Strong protection of proprietary information would be a prerequisite for use.
- A credible early pilot could run in parallel with the company's existing
  process, followed by review of Greenlit's output by counsel.
- Testing against a completed public dossier would not be persuasive because the
  model would effectively have the answer. A better validation would use the
  underlying artifacts while withholding the final dossier.
- Ryan explicitly suggested contacting Tony Pavel and Perumal Gandhi and gave
  permission to mention his referral.

## Interpretation

These are Greenlit interpretations, not direct claims from Ryan:

- The initial wedge may be submission readiness and evidence orchestration, with
  drafting downstream of that structured foundation.
- The product should complement counsel and reduce avoidable assembly work while
  leaving final legal judgment, representation, and submission to counsel.
- Time savings alone may be weaker than risk reduction, readiness visibility,
  and avoidance of late-discovered evidence gaps.
- A design partner with both source artifacts and a completed historical dossier
  could enable a useful blinded reconstruction exercise.

## Open questions

- Who owns submission readiness inside an active company?
- Which coordination failures recur across less successful submissions?
- What portion of outside-counsel expense is document assembly versus expert judgment?
- Would counsel accept, resist, or prefer a structured preliminary package?
- What security controls must be present before a company will upload process IP?
- What artifact set is sufficient to reconstruct a credible dossier without seeing the final submission?
